The CPT began, fittingly for a test now dominant in delta soils, in the Netherlands:

* **1930s — The Dutch mechanical cone.** P. Barentsen and colleagues developed the first "deep sounding" apparatus: a 10 cm² cone pushed by hand rods, reading cone resistance mechanically at the surface. The method spread quickly through the Dutch practice of founding structures on piles driven through soft ground.
* **1950s — The friction sleeve.** Begemann's adhesion-jacket cone (1953) added the sleeve friction measurement, and with it the friction ratio — the key that unlocked soil-type identification from penetration data alone.
* **1960s — The electric cone.** Load cells inside the cone (developed strongly by Delft and Fugro) replaced mechanical readings: continuous profiles, better accuracy, no rod-friction ambiguity. The modern CPT was born.
* **1980s — The piezocone (CPTU).** Adding pore-pressure measurement (usually at the u₂ position behind the cone) transformed interpretation: corrected cone resistance q<sub>t</sub>, drainage assessment, and dissipation tests for consolidation properties.
* **Mid-1980s — The seismic cone (SCPT).** Geophones in the cone (Campanella & Robertson) added shear-wave velocity — small-strain stiffness G₀ from the same sounding.
* **Today.** The CPT/CPTU is standardised internationally (ISO 22476-1), performed from trucks, crawlers, jack-up rigs, and seabed frames offshore, with variants including full-flow penetrometers (T-bar, ball) for very soft clays, and free-fall penetrometers. It has largely displaced the SPT as the premier in-situ test wherever soils allow penetration.
