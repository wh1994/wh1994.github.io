*A bibliometric look at the International Journal of Physical Modelling in Geotechnics, 2001–2026*

Physical modelling — reproducing geotechnical problems at reduced scale, most famously in geotechnical centrifuges — occupies a special place in geotechnical engineering. It sits between theory and the field: more controlled than a case history, more real than a simulation. Since 2001, the field has had a dedicated home in the *International Journal of Physical Modelling in Geotechnics* (IJPMG), founded under the auspices of the ISSMGE technical committee on physical modelling (TC2, now TC104).

To mark a quarter-century of the journal, I assembled its complete publication record — all 488 items ever published, from Volume 1, Issue 1 (2001) to the current Volume 26 (2026) — including titles, authors, abstracts, keywords, institutional affiliations and citation counts. After setting aside editorials, referee lists, discussions and other non-research items, the analysed corpus comprises **429 research papers by authors from 48 countries and over 400 institutions**. The dataset was compiled from the OpenAlex and Crossref scholarly databases and completed by retrieving early-volume metadata directly from the journal archive; details are given in the methods note at the end.

What emerges is not just the story of one journal, but a quantitative portrait of how an entire experimental discipline has shifted its attention — away from its earthquake-engineering roots and towards the energy transition and climate-driven problems — and how its geography has been redrawn along the way.

## A journal's life in numbers

![Annual research output of IJPMG, 2001–2026](/images/posts/ijpmg-25-years/fig1_annual_output.svg)

*Figure 1. Annual research output of IJPMG, 2001–2026.*

The journal's output history is itself a piece of disciplinary history. IJPMG launched strongly in 2001 with 25 research papers, riding the momentum of the international centrifuge-modelling community that had been meeting since the 1980s. Output then declined through the mid-2000s to a genuinely precarious low: **in 2008 the journal published a single research paper**, and Volume 10 (2010) contains only two issues. This was no indexing artefact — the archive really is that thin.

The turning point came in 2011, when the journal was relaunched under ICE Publishing with a rejuvenated editorial structure. Output recovered steadily, spiked in 2015 (a year swollen by special-issue and conference-linked material), and has since stabilised at a healthy 20–30 papers per year. In 2018 the journal expanded from four to six issues per year, and in 2025 it moved to Emerald Publishing, where the full archive now lives. The 2026 figure in the chart covers only a partial year — with three issues published by August, the journal is on pace for its strongest year in a decade.

The lesson of the 2006–2010 trough is worth pausing on: niche experimental journals live and die by the health of their community infrastructure. The relaunch coincided with renewed investment in centrifuge facilities worldwide and with TC104's push to make physical modelling data citable and shareable. The journal's recovery tracked the field's.

## What the field studies — and how that has changed

Each paper was assigned to one or more of twelve themes by matching its title, abstract and keywords against theme-specific vocabularies (a paper about seismic response of monopiles counts toward both "earthquake" and "offshore"). The result is a map of the field's attention across five periods.

![Share of papers touching each theme, by period](/images/posts/ijpmg-25-years/fig2_theme_heatmap.svg)

*Figure 2. Share of papers touching each theme, by period.*

Three things stand out.

**The constants.** Piles and deep foundations are the evergreen core of physical modelling, involved in roughly 30% of papers in every period — unsurprising, since pile–soil interaction is precisely the kind of problem where stress-level similitude matters and centrifuge modelling excels. Just as constant, and more telling, is the fifth of the journal devoted to *how* to model: instrumentation, imaging and measurement (about 25% throughout) and testing methods and scaling laws (18–39%). IJPMG is, to a large degree, a journal about the craft of experimentation itself. The single most cited paper in its history — Garnier et al.'s 2007 catalogue of scaling laws and similitude questions, with over 500 citations — is exactly such a paper.

**The decline.** Earthquake and liquefaction studies dominated the early journal, touching a quarter to a third of all papers in 2001–2010. That share has fallen to under 20%. This reflects maturity rather than abandonment: the great international liquefaction programmes of the 1990s (VELACS and successors) had made dynamic centrifuge testing a centrepiece of the field, and much of that agenda has since been consolidated into design practice. The theme is ticking up again in the 2020s — now often in new guises such as fault-rupture interaction with infrastructure and seismic metamaterials.

![The big movers: themes with the largest change in share](/images/posts/ijpmg-25-years/fig3_big_movers.svg)

*Figure 3. The big movers: themes with the largest change in share.*

**The rise.** The unmistakable story of the last fifteen years is offshore energy. Papers touching offshore and wind-energy problems — monopiles, suction caissons, anchors, moorings, jack-ups, cables and pipelines — made up about 6% of the journal in its first decade. Since 2011 they have consistently accounted for over 20%, making this the field's largest growth area by a wide margin. The driver is obvious: the offshore wind industry needed design methods for enormous, cyclically loaded foundations in soils where experience was thin, and centrifuge modelling could deliver validated soil–structure interaction data faster and more cheaply than offshore trials. It is a textbook case of an experimental discipline pivoting to meet an industrial demand.

Two quieter climbs deserve mention. Slopes, dams and embankments grew from a marginal 2% in the journal's first years to 17% in the 2020s, increasingly framed around rainfall-induced landslides, debris flows and dam failure — climate-adjacent hazards. And the "unsaturated soils and climate effects" theme (rainfall infiltration, desiccation, freeze–thaw, thermal behaviour, energy geostructures) has tripled from ~5% to ~14%. Physical modelling is following the profession's centre of gravity: from building new infrastructure to protecting existing infrastructure against a changing climate.

## Who writes the journal: a geography redrawn

![Top 15 contributing countries, 2001–2026](/images/posts/ijpmg-25-years/fig4_top_countries.svg)

*Figure 4. Top 15 contributing countries, 2001–2026.*

Aggregated over 25 years, the league table looks familiar for an Anglophone journal with strong centrifuge traditions: the United Kingdom (80 papers), Australia (74), the United States (60), Japan (58) and China (51) lead, with a long tail of 43 further countries. But the aggregate hides the real story, which is in the period-by-period shares.

![Country shares by period](/images/posts/ijpmg-25-years/fig5_country_share_by_era.svg)

*Figure 5. Country shares of contributions, by period.*

- **Japan's founding role.** In 2001–2005 Japan was the journal's second-largest contributor (19% of country mentions), reflecting the deep Japanese centrifuge tradition — the journal's first editor, Osamu Kusakabe, wrote the opening editorial of Volume 1. That share has since fallen to 5%, as Japanese physical modellers increasingly publish in *Soils and Foundations* and elsewhere.
- **The UK–Australia axis** carried the journal through its middle years, powered by two institutions above all: the University of Western Australia (57 papers — the most prolific institution in the journal's history, home of the drum centrifuge and offshore geotechnics powerhouse COFS) and the University of Cambridge (34 papers, the Schofield Centre).
- **China's arrival** is the defining shift of the current decade. Essentially absent before 2010, China is now the single largest contributor (18% of mentions in 2021–2026), on the back of major centrifuge investments at Zhejiang, Tongji, Tsinghua and the Nanjing Hydraulic Research Institute.
- **The broadening base.** Perhaps the healthiest signal is the growth of "Other": countries outside the top seven now supply nearly 40% of contributions. Iran, India, Brazil (via UFRJ's strong offshore programme) and a widening set of European groups (Norway, the Netherlands, France's Université Gustave Eiffel with its historic LCPC centrifuge) all feature regularly. A field once concentrated in perhaps a dozen laboratories is visibly democratising — aided by cheaper instrumentation, shared facilities and the growth of well-instrumented 1-g and small-centrifuge testing.

![Most prolific institutions](/images/posts/ijpmg-25-years/fig7_top_institutions.svg)

*Figure 6. The most prolific contributing institutions.*

The institutional table underlines how capital-intensive the field remains: every name on it operates (or operated) a major centrifuge facility. Physical modelling is "big science" at laboratory scale, and its bibliometrics behave accordingly — output concentrates where the machines are.

## Collaboration is rising

![International co-authorship rate by period](/images/posts/ijpmg-25-years/fig6_international_collaboration.svg)

*Figure 7. International co-authorship rate, by period.*

Roughly a quarter of IJPMG papers had authors from two or more countries through the journal's first two decades. In the 2020s that figure has climbed to **34%**, and the average author count per paper has grown from ~3.6 to ~4.4. Both trends mirror science-wide patterns, but in this field they also have a concrete driver: transnational facility-sharing. Programmes explicitly designed to give researchers access to other countries' centrifuges — European transnational-access schemes and bilateral arrangements around large facilities — turn facility scarcity into collaboration. The journal's rising co-authorship is the paper trail of that shared infrastructure.

## Indications: where the field is heading

Reading the corpus as a whole, five indications stand out.

1. **The energy transition is now the field's largest client.** Offshore wind alone sustains nearly a quarter of the journal's output, and the current volumes add shared-mooring anchors, energy piles and submarine-landslide impacts on turbine foundations. Physical modelling has successfully re-tooled itself once; the growing pipelines of floating wind, hydrogen storage and offshore decommissioning suggest that demand will not slacken.

2. **Climate adaptation is the quiet second engine.** Rainfall-and-earthquake combined loading of embankments, dam overtopping and internal erosion, permafrost and desiccation problems, scour — themes that barely registered in 2001 now jointly account for a fifth of the journal. Expect this share to keep growing as ageing flood-defence and dam infrastructure meets intensifying hydrological extremes.

3. **The methods core is renewing itself.** The constant ~25% of instrumentation-and-methods papers is now absorbing a new wave: machine-learning analysis of model-test imagery, fibre-optic sensing, and "modelling-of-models" validation of new devices appear repeatedly in the 2025–2026 issues. The craft literature that produced the 2007 scaling-laws catalogue is being rewritten for a data-rich era — a shift worth watching, because whoever writes the reference methods papers of this generation will collect the next 500-citation paper.

4. **The community's centre of mass is moving east and south.** China's rise from zero to largest contributor within fifteen years is the sharpest geographic shift, but the growth of the long tail matters more for the journal's future: a discipline once definable by a dozen laboratories now takes contributions from 48 countries, and the "Other" category is its fastest-growing constituency.

5. **Collaboration is becoming the norm, not the exception.** With international co-authorship at a third of papers and rising, and facility-access programmes formalising the sharing of expensive infrastructure, the sociology of the field increasingly resembles that of large-instrument physics — a change with implications for how early-career researchers enter the field, and for how facilities justify their funding.

## Methods and data

The dataset covers every item published in IJPMG from Volume 1 (2001) to Volume 26, Issue 3 (2026): 488 records in total, retrieved from the OpenAlex and Crossref APIs (which agree exactly on the count) and keyed by DOI. Titles, authors, abstracts, keywords, affiliations, volume/issue and citation counts come primarily from OpenAlex. Fifty-eight non-research items (editorials, annual referee lists, award announcements, discussions/closures, errata and publisher notes) were excluded, as was one phantom database record for a manuscript that was never actually published, leaving 429 research papers. Thirty-four papers — mostly from the 2001–2010 Thomas Telford/ICE era — lacked affiliations or abstracts in the databases; these were completed from the journal's archive pages, giving the analysed corpus full coverage of author countries and abstracts.

Countries are counted with full counting (a paper with authors from *n* countries counts once toward each), so period shares are shares of country *mentions*. Themes are assigned by regular-expression matching of curated term lists against each paper's title, abstract and keywords; themes are not mutually exclusive, so shares within a period do not sum to 100%. Keyword data are OpenAlex-assigned concepts supplemented by author keywords for the archive-scraped papers. Citation counts are OpenAlex values as of 7 August 2026 and, as always, favour older papers. 2026 is a partial publication year (three of six issues at the time of writing).
