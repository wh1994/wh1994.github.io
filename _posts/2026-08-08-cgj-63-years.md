*A bibliometric look at the Canadian Geotechnical Journal, 1963–2026*

When the National Research Council of Canada launched the *Canadian Geotechnical Journal* in 1963, its mandate was distinctly national: a home for the engineering problems of a country built on Leda clay, muskeg and permafrost. Sixty-three years later the journal — now published by Canadian Science Publishing — is one of the largest general geotechnics journals in the world, and its pages record two transformations at once: the field's own evolution, and the journal's journey from a Canadian institution to a global one in which Canada itself is no longer the largest contributor.

To take stock of that history, I assembled the journal's complete publication record — every item from Volume 1 (1963) to the current Volume 63 (2026) — including titles, authors, abstracts, keywords, institutional affiliations and citation counts. After setting aside discussions, replies, book reviews, editorials, errata and other non-research material (1,058 items), the analysed corpus comprises **6,184 research papers**, which together have accumulated roughly **280,000 citations**. The dataset was compiled from the OpenAlex and Crossref scholarly databases; details — including an important limitation of the pre-2008 affiliation record — are given in the methods note at the end.

What emerges is the portrait of a journal that grew steadily for five decades and then surged; whose scientific centre of gravity moved from the field and the site investigation to the laboratory and the computer; and whose authorship has been comprehensively internationalised within a single academic generation.

## A journal's life in numbers

![Annual research output of the Canadian Geotechnical Journal, 1963–2026](/images/posts/cgj-63-years/fig1_annual_output.svg)

*Figure 1. Annual research output of the Canadian Geotechnical Journal, 1963–2026.*

The output curve is one of almost uninterrupted growth. From 4 papers in the founding year and around 20 per year in the late 1960s, the journal reached roughly 50 papers a year through the 1970s, 70–90 in the 1980s and 1990s, and 100–130 in the 2000s. A further step up came in the mid-2010s (150–180 papers a year), and the last few years have been explosive: 212 papers in 2024 and an all-time record of **264 in 2025** — a five-fold increase over the journal's 1970s output. With 204 papers already published by August, 2026 is on pace to break the record again. In research-paper volume, the CGJ now publishes roughly twice as much as *Géotechnique*.

One archival footnote matters for anyone using the journal's records: several hundred papers from the late 1990s and 2000s were registered under two DOI schemes (e.g. `10.1139/t99-039` and `10.1139/cgj-36-4-625` are the same 1999 paper), with citations split between the twin records. This analysis merges 496 such duplicates.

## What the field studies — and how that has changed

Each paper was assigned to one or more of seventeen themes by matching its title, abstract and keywords against theme-specific vocabularies (a paper on the seismic response of piles counts toward both "earthquake" and "piles"). Because themes overlap, shares within a decade do not sum to 100%.

![Share of papers touching each theme, by decade](/images/posts/cgj-63-years/fig2_theme_heatmap.svg)

*Figure 2. Share of papers touching each theme, by decade.*

**The constants.** The journal's backbone has barely moved in sixty years: groundwater, seepage and pore-pressure problems (24–32% in every decade — the highest sustained share of any theme, fitting for the journal that published Fredlund's permeability functions), embankments, dams and ground improvement (18–24% throughout, from muskeg construction in the 1960s to tailings dams today), and laboratory testing (18–27%). Piles, footings and retaining structures have likewise held steady shares. As with its British counterpart, the applications have not changed; the methods of attack have.

![The big movers: themes with the largest change in share](/images/posts/cgj-63-years/fig3_big_movers.svg)

*Figure 3. The big movers: themes with the largest change in share.*

**The two great risers.** The same twin ascent that reshaped *Géotechnique* is visible here, with the computer slightly ahead of the constitutive model. Numerical and analytical methods grew from 6% of the journal in the 1960s to 36% by the 2000s — where they plateaued: the finite element method, probabilistic analysis and, lately, machine learning are now simply part of the furniture. Constitutive and soil-behaviour themes rose in near-lockstep, from 10% to 30%, and are still climbing — the 2020s value is the theme's all-time high, driven by micromechanics, DEM and unsaturated-soil constitutive frameworks.

**The Canadian signature — and its decline.** No theme is more identified with the CGJ than cold-regions engineering, and no theme has fallen further. Permafrost, frozen ground and frost-heave research made up 10% of the journal at its founding and peaked near **15% in the 1970s** — the decade of the Mackenzie Valley pipeline inquiry, when Arctic geotechnics was a national project and the journal was its house organ. The theme then slid for four straight decades, to just 3% in the 2010s, as the megaprojects wound down. The 2020s show the first uptick in half a century (5%) — climate change is thawing the permafrost, and with it, reviving the field that studies it.

**The other movers.**

- *Earthquake and liquefaction* (2% in the 1960s, 14% today) climbed steadily through the 1980s and 1990s — the era of the Canadian Liquefaction Experiment and the seismic reassessment of west-coast infrastructure — and has held its share since.
- *Tunnels and underground* (2% → 13%) is the quiet riser of the last two decades, tracking the global urban-tunnelling boom and the arrival of Chinese authorship (see below).
- *Environmental and bio-geotechnics* (1% → 14%) barely existed before 1980; landfill and contaminant-transport research built the theme in the 1990s, and tailings, bio-cementation and sustainability are its current engines.
- *Unsaturated soils and climate effects* deserves special mention: 24% of papers in the 2020s, its all-time high. The journal is the historical home of unsaturated soil mechanics — the Fredlund school at Saskatchewan above all — and the theme's rise from 10% in the 1970s has been relentless.
- *Slopes and landslides* peaked early (25% in the 1980s, reflecting the sensitive-clay flowslides of Québec and the rock-slope programmes of the west) and has eased to 16% — still among the journal's largest themes.

## Who writes the journal: a geography transformed

A caveat first: affiliation metadata for the CGJ was only systematically deposited from **2008** onward — before then, neither OpenAlex nor Crossref records who wrote from where (coverage is under 5%). The geography analysis therefore covers 2008–2026: 2,983 papers, authors from 91 countries and some 1,700 institutions. The journal's first 45 years cannot be mapped this way, though its Canadian dominance in that era is not in doubt.

![Top 15 contributing countries, 2008–2026](/images/posts/cgj-63-years/fig4_top_countries.svg)

*Figure 4. Top 15 contributing countries, 2008–2026 (full counting).*

Even within this recent window, the ranking is startling for a journal with "Canadian" on the cover: **China leads with 870 papers to Canada's 726** (a further 290 carry Hong Kong affiliations), with the United States (527), Australia (376) and the United Kingdom (288) following. The trend within the window is more striking still:

![Country shares since 2008](/images/posts/cgj-63-years/fig5_country_share_by_period.svg)

*Figure 5. Where the papers come from: country shares since 2008.*

- **2008–13:** Canada was clearly the largest contributor (23% of country mentions), with the US second (14%) and China fourth (8%).
- **2014–19:** Canada (18%) barely held off China (15%).
- **2020–26:** China has 27% — **more than double Canada's 12%** — and greater China (with Hong Kong's 7%) accounts for a third of the journal's current output.

This is the same eastward shift documented across the geotechnical literature (China also overtook the UK in *Géotechnique* in the 2020s), but nowhere is it more dramatic than here: the CGJ has effectively become an international journal whose largest customer base is Chinese, publishing on tunnels, numerical methods and soil behaviour. Canada's decline in share is not a decline in papers — Canadian output per year is roughly stable — it is dilution by growth: the journal's recent expansion has been almost entirely non-Canadian.

![Most prolific institutions, 2008–2026](/images/posts/cgj-63-years/fig7_top_institutions.svg)

*Figure 6. Most prolific institutions, 2008–2026.*

The institutional table tells the same story in miniature. The most prolific institution since 2008 is not Canadian but the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (152 papers); Queen's University (115) is the top Canadian contributor, ahead of Western, Laval, UBC and Alberta. Zhejiang, Tongji and Hohai represent the Chinese cohort; the University of Western Australia (113) and the National University of Singapore carry the offshore- and centrifuge-modelling traditions. The Norwegian Geotechnical Institute (51) rounds out a list that would have been unrecognisable to the journal's founders.

## From lone authors to global teams

![Team size and international collaboration](/images/posts/cgj-63-years/fig6_teams_collaboration.svg)

*Figure 7. Team size, 1963–2026, and international co-authorship since 2008.*

In the journal's first decade, half of all papers (49%) had a single author and the average paper had 1.5 authors. Every decade since has moved away from that world, and the change has accelerated: the mean paper now has **4.2 authors**, and only 2% are single-authored. International co-authorship, measurable from 2008, has climbed from 31% (2008–13) to **48% (2020–26)** — nearly half the journal now crosses a border. Both trends mirror science-wide patterns, amplified by the field's economics: centrifuges, advanced laboratories and long-term field monitoring are shared infrastructure, and shared infrastructure produces shared authorship.

## What gets cited

The journal's most-cited papers are dominated, to a degree unusual among geotechnical journals, by two bodies of work: unsaturated soil mechanics and the cone penetration test.

Four of the top ten belong to the unsaturated-soils canon built by Del Fredlund and colleagues at Saskatchewan — the journal is, by citation, the world's reference archive for the soil-water characteristic curve. Three more belong to Peter Robertson's CPT series, the everyday toolkit of practising engineers on every continent. Add Meyerhof's bearing capacity (published in the journal's very first volume), Hungr's landslide-runout model and Phoon & Kulhawy's reliability framework, and the list reads as the practical half of the graduate syllabus — where *Géotechnique*'s classics supplied the theory (Bishop's slip circle, Bolton's dilatancy, Cundall & Strack's DEM), the CGJ's supplied the design methods.

## What it adds up to

Three conclusions stand out from the corpus.

**First, the journal outgrew its founding identity — twice.** The CGJ began as the journal of Canadian ground: muskeg, Leda clay, permafrost. By the 1990s it had become a general international geotechnics journal that happened to be Canadian; by the 2020s it is a global journal in which Canada supplies one paper in eight. The near-disappearance and tentative return of permafrost research traces the same arc in a single theme.

**Second, the intellectual transformation matches its peers.** The rise of numerical methods (6% → 36%) and constitutive soil behaviour (10% → 30%), the arrival of earthquake engineering, environmental geotechnics and urban tunnelling, and the steadiness of the field's applied backbone are near-identical to the patterns in *Géotechnique* — evidence that these are properties of the discipline, not of any one journal's editorial policy.

**Third, the sociology has changed as fast as the science.** A journal of lone authors (49% single-authored in the 1960s) is now a journal of four-person, frequently transnational teams (48% international). The paper as the record of one engineer's insight has given way to the paper as the output of a networked laboratory.

The Canadian Geotechnical Journal's founders set out to serve a country's engineering needs. Sixty-three years and six thousand papers later, the archive shows they built something their discipline needed more: a journal of record whose story — nationally rooted, globally overtaken, computationally transformed — is the story of geotechnical engineering itself.

### Methods note

**Data.** All records for ISSN 0008-3674 / 1208-6010 were harvested from OpenAlex (7,738 works) and Crossref (7,638 works) in August 2026 and merged by DOI. Abstracts were reconstructed from the OpenAlex inverted index, with Crossref JATS abstracts as fallback. Research papers were separated from discussions, replies, closures, book reviews, editorials, errata and other non-research material using title patterns (the CGJ registers discussions as "*Original title*: Discussion") and publisher type fields; 1,058 items were set aside. Duplicate DOI registrations — the same paper deposited under two DOI schemes with citations split between records — were merged on normalised title and volume (496 removed, citations summed).

**Themes.** Papers were matched against seventeen keyword vocabularies applied to concatenated title, abstract, keyword and concept text. A paper may count toward several themes; shares therefore do not sum to 100%.

**Geography.** Author affiliations for CGJ content were only deposited to the scholarly databases from ~2008 onward; before then coverage is below 5%, and no country-level analysis is attempted. From 2008 coverage exceeds 97% (a transitional 2007 sits at ~50%). Country counting is full counting: a paper with authors in *n* countries counts once for each; Hong Kong affiliations are tallied separately from mainland China's in the figures and are labelled as China (Hong Kong). Citation counts are OpenAlex, August 2026, and favour recent decades' citing literature.

**Caveats.** Theme vocabularies are heuristic and English-language; early-era titles are terse, and theme shares before 1970 (145 papers) carry wide uncertainty. Institution names reflect OpenAlex normalisation. The 2026 year is partial (through early August).
