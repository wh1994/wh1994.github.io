*A bibliometric look at Géotechnique, 1948–2026*

When the Institution of Civil Engineers published the first issue of *Géotechnique* in June 1948, soil mechanics was barely twenty years old as an organised discipline. The journal that emerged — international in outlook from its French-accented name onward — became the field's journal of record: the place where the slip circle, the pore-pressure coefficients, critical state soil mechanics, the state parameter and the discrete element method were first set down in print.

To take stock of that history, I assembled the journal's complete publication record — every item from Volume 1 (1948) to the current Volume 76 (2026) — including titles, authors, abstracts, keywords, institutional affiliations and citation counts. After setting aside discussions, book reviews, editorials, obituaries and other non-research material (968 items — *Géotechnique*'s famous discussion culture accounts for most of them), the analysed corpus comprises **4,371 research papers by authors from 79 countries and over 1,400 institutions**, which together have accumulated over 340,000 citations. The dataset was compiled from the OpenAlex and Crossref scholarly databases; details, including the affiliation-coverage limits of the early archive, are given in the methods note at the end.

What emerges is a quantitative portrait of a discipline growing up: from a small, largely British club of individual authors writing about seepage and slip circles, to a global, collaborative science whose centre of gravity is shifting east — and whose intellectual core has been rebuilt twice, first by the constitutive-modelling revolution and then by the computer.

## A journal's life in numbers

![Annual research output of Géotechnique, 1948–2026](/images/posts/geotechnique-78-years/fig1_annual_output.svg)

*Figure 1. Annual research output of Géotechnique, 1948–2026.*

The output curve tells a story of steady institutional growth punctuated by deliberate expansions. The journal published around a dozen research papers a year in its first decade, roughly 20–50 per year through the 1960s–1980s, and 40–60 in the 1990s. The step change came around 2000, as the journal expanded from four issues a year (to 1997) to six in 1998, ten in 2001 and twelve from 2010: output doubled within a few years and has since fluctuated between roughly 75 and 130 papers annually. The 2015 peak (133 papers) reflects the *Géotechnique Symposium in Print* and a cluster of themed issues; the 2026 figure covers only a partial year — with 52 papers by August, the journal is on pace for a typical modern year.

Two publishing-history footnotes matter for anyone using the archive. First, the entire back catalogue was double-registered under two DOI schemes during the ICE digitisation era — the same paper often exists as two database records with citations split between them (this analysis merges 400 such duplicates). Second, in 2025 publishing moved from ICE Publishing to Emerald, where the full archive now lives.

## What the field studies — and how that has changed

Each paper was assigned to one or more of sixteen themes by matching its title, abstract and keywords against theme-specific vocabularies (a paper on the seismic response of monopiles counts toward both "earthquake" and "offshore"). Because themes overlap, shares within a decade do not sum to 100%. The result is a map of the discipline's attention across eight decades.

![Share of papers touching each theme, by decade](/images/posts/geotechnique-78-years/fig2_theme_heatmap.svg)

*Figure 2. Share of papers touching each theme, by decade.*

**The constants.** Some things have anchored the journal throughout: laboratory testing (14% of papers in the first decade, 30% in the 2020s — the triaxial apparatus is as central to the field's identity as ever) and the cluster of groundwater, seepage and pore-pressure problems (steadily 18–31%, and at its historical *maximum* now, driven by internal erosion, suffusion and climate-related seepage problems). Slopes, foundations, retaining structures and embankments — the bread-and-butter applications — have each held remarkably stable shares for seventy years. The discipline's applications have not changed; how it attacks them has.

![The big movers: themes with the largest change in share](/images/posts/geotechnique-78-years/fig3_big_movers.svg)

*Figure 3. The big movers: themes with the largest change in share.*

**The two great risers.** The defining transformation of the corpus is the twin ascent of constitutive modelling and computation. Papers touching soil behaviour and constitutive themes — critical state, plasticity, dilatancy, anisotropy, micromechanics — grew from 6% of the journal in 1948–59 to **43% in the 2020s**, the largest theme in every decade since the 1980s. The inflection is exactly where the textbooks put it: Roscoe, Schofield and Wroth's *On the Yielding of Soils* (1958) seeded the critical-state programme that Cambridge built through the 1960s, and the theme's share more than doubled between the 1950s and 1970s. Numerical and analytical methods track the same curve one step behind (3% → 36%), as the finite element method arrived in the 1970s and became routine. Together these two themes mark the journal's evolution from a repository of case records and testing techniques into the field's theoretical engine room.

**The decliner.** Rock mechanics is the one theme that rose and then fell: from 7% at the founding to ~15% in the 1960s–70s — the era when engineering rock mechanics was being invented and had nowhere else to publish — then steadily down to 7% as the *International Journal of Rock Mechanics and Mining Sciences* (1964) and its successors gave the subject its own homes.

**The new arrivals.** Three themes that barely existed in the journal's first quarter-century now jointly touch half of it:

- *Earthquake and liquefaction* (1% before 1970, 18% in the 2020s), which entered the journal in the 1970s after the Niigata and Alaska earthquakes made liquefaction a defining problem and has climbed ever since.
- *Offshore and energy* (essentially zero before 1970, 16% since 2010). The North Sea platforms of the 1970s–80s brought the first wave; offshore wind brought the second and larger one — monopiles, suction caissons and anchors are now among the journal's most frequent subjects, joined by energy piles, gas hydrates and nuclear-waste disposal.
- *Environmental and bio-geotechnics* — the fastest-growing theme right now. Negligible before 1980 and still under 5% through the 2010s, it has **more than doubled to 11% in the 2020s** on the back of bio-cementation (MICP), biopolymers, tailings, recycled materials and sustainability-framed research. If the 2010s belonged to offshore wind, the current decade's marginal growth belongs to biology and the circular economy.

The unsaturated-soils and climate-effects theme completes the picture, tripling from ~5% (1970s) to ~20% — rainfall-induced failure, desiccation, freeze–thaw and thermal problems now sit squarely in the mainstream, another signal of the profession's turn toward climate adaptation.

## Who writes the journal: a geography redrawn

![Top 15 contributing countries, 1948–2026](/images/posts/geotechnique-78-years/fig4_top_countries.svg)

*Figure 4. Top 15 contributing countries, 1948–2026.*

Aggregated over 78 years, the league table is what you would expect of a journal published in London: the United Kingdom leads with 1,335 papers — more than the next two countries combined — followed by the United States (564), Australia (435), China (321) and Canada (199). But the aggregate flattens the real story, which is in the decade-by-decade shares.

![Country shares by decade](/images/posts/geotechnique-78-years/fig5_country_share_by_era.svg)

*Figure 5. Country shares of papers, by decade.*

- **The British century.** Through the 1960s–80s the UK supplied 39–47% of country mentions, with the US a steady second (15–18%). *Géotechnique* in this era was to a first approximation the house journal of British soil mechanics — Imperial College, Cambridge and the Building Research Station above all — with a strong Commonwealth and North American penumbra.
- **The long broadening.** From the 1990s the UK share erodes steadily: 33%, 29%, 19%, 17%. Nothing dramatic happened to British geotechnics; the rest of the world simply arrived. Italy, Japan and China (Hong Kong) rose in the 1990s; Australia's share doubled in the 2010s (12%, powered by the offshore boom and the University of Western Australia).
- **China's arrival** is, as everywhere in science, the defining shift of the current decade: from under 1% before 2000 to 9% in the 2010s to **17.5% in the 2020s — now the journal's largest contributor**, a hair ahead of the UK (17.0%). Add Hong Kong's 7% and China as a whole accounts for a quarter of current output. The institutional table below already shows two Hong Kong universities in the all-time top seven.
- **The American paradox.** The US, second in every decade from the 1940s to the 2000s, has drifted from 17% to 8% — reflecting less a decline of American geotechnics than the gravitational pull of its own journals (the ASCE *Journal of Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Engineering* above all).

![Most prolific institutions](/images/posts/geotechnique-78-years/fig7_top_institutions.svg)

*Figure 6. Most prolific institutions, 1948–2026.*

The all-time institutional table is a history of the discipline in miniature: Imperial College (270 papers) and Cambridge (186) — the two poles of British soil mechanics, effective-stress empiricism and critical-state theory respectively — followed by the University of Western Australia (176), whose offshore geotechnics centre made it the journal's most prolific non-UK contributor, and Oxford (115), whose rise is comparatively recent and closely tied to offshore renewables.

## From lone authors to global teams

![International co-authorship and team size by decade](/images/posts/geotechnique-78-years/fig6_international_collaboration.svg)

*Figure 7. International co-authorship and team size, by decade.*

In the 1950s, 65% of *Géotechnique* papers had a single author, and the mean was 1.2 authors per paper — Skempton, Bishop and their generation wrote alone. Every decade since has moved monotonically away from that world: the average paper now has **4.0 authors**, and only 3% are single-authored. International co-authorship has followed the same curve with a lag: 13% of papers in the 1970s had authors from two or more countries; in the 2020s, **55% do** — the majority of the journal is now internationally co-authored. Both trends mirror science-wide patterns, but the field's own economics amplify them: centrifuges, advanced laboratories and large field campaigns are shared infrastructure, and shared infrastructure produces shared authorship.

Cundall and Strack's 1979 paper on the discrete element method is in a class of its own — at 17,000 citations it is not just the journal's most-cited paper but one of the most-cited papers in all of engineering, cited today far beyond geotechnics wherever granular materials are simulated. The rest of the list divides neatly into the analysis methods every practitioner uses (Bishop, Newmark, Morgenstern–Price, Griffiths–Lane), the constitutive landmarks (Bolton's dilatancy relations, the Barcelona model of Alonso–Gens–Josa, Been and Jefferies' state parameter, and — just outside the top ten — Roscoe, Schofield and Wroth's 1958 *On the Yielding of Soils* and Skempton's 1954 pore-pressure coefficients), and the experimental methods papers (White–Take–Bolton's PIV). Eighteen papers in the corpus have passed 1,000 citations; 861 have passed 100.

## Indications: where the field is heading

Reading the corpus as a whole, five indications stand out.

1. **Theory and computation have won the argument — and set the next one.** With constitutive/soil-behaviour themes at 43% and numerical methods at 36%, the journal's centre is now unambiguously model-building. The open question the current volumes pose is whether machine learning (already visible in the keyword data) becomes the third methodological wave, and whether it will produce a landmark of Cundall–Strack rank or remain a fitting tool.

2. **The energy transition is the field's largest external client.** Offshore and energy themes have sustained ~16% of the journal for fifteen years, and their composition is shifting from oil-and-gas-era problems to wind, anchors for floating structures, energy geostructures and underground storage. There is no sign of saturation.

3. **Biology is the newest frontier.** Environmental and bio-geotechnics doubling to 11% in a single decade is the sharpest thematic acceleration in the journal's recent history. Bio-cementation, biopolymers and vegetation effects are moving from novelty to mainstream, and mine tailings and re-used materials are bringing geotechnics into the sustainability economy.

4. **The geography will keep tilting east.** China moved from nowhere to first place in twenty-five years; with Hong Kong included, it already produces a quarter of the journal. The interesting question for a London-published journal with a French name is not whether this continues but how the journal's institutional culture — the discussion tradition, the Rankine Lecture canon — absorbs it.

5. **The lone author is extinct.** From 65% single-authored papers in the 1950s to 3% today, and from 1.2 to 4.0 authors per paper, *Géotechnique* now behaves sociologically like a laboratory science. The 55% international co-authorship rate — majority-international for the first time in the 2020s — suggests the discipline's community, like its problems, no longer respects national boundaries.

## Methods and data

The dataset covers every item published in *Géotechnique* from Volume 1 (1948) to Volume 76 (2026), retrieved from the OpenAlex and Crossref APIs and keyed by DOI. The raw pull returned 5,740 OpenAlex records; removing 400 duplicate DOI registrations (the ICE-era archive was deposited under two DOI schemes, so many papers exist as two records — these were merged on normalised title and volume, with citation counts summed) and a handful of misattributed foreign records leaves 5,339 items. Of these, 968 non-research items (discussions and closures — the largest category by far, reflecting the journal's discussion tradition — plus book reviews, editorials, corrigenda, obituaries, referee lists and contents pages) were excluded by rule-based classification of titles and database type fields, leaving **4,371 research papers**.

Titles, authors, abstracts, keywords, affiliations and citation counts come primarily from OpenAlex, supplemented by Crossref. Affiliation coverage is the dataset's main limitation and varies by publishing era: it is near-complete for the 2020s (99%), good for the 1970s–1990s (87–89%), lower for the 2000s and 2010s (70–80%, concentrated in 2001–2004 and 2010–2015 deposit gaps), and essentially absent for the 1950s (2%), which is why country and collaboration figures begin in the 1960s. Around 180 papers' countries were recovered by matching raw affiliation strings against a gazetteer of institutions and country names. Countries are counted with full counting (a paper with authors from *n* countries counts once toward each), so decade shares are shares of country *mentions*.

Themes are assigned by regular-expression matching of curated term lists against each paper's title, abstract, keywords and database-assigned concepts; themes are not mutually exclusive, so shares within a decade do not sum to 100%. Pre-1990 papers are matched mainly on titles and (where available) abstracts, which slightly understates early theme shares. Citation counts are OpenAlex values as of 8 August 2026 and, as always, favour older papers. 2026 is a partial publication year (52 research papers at the time of writing).
