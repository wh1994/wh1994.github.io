*A bibliometric look at ASCE's Journal of Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Engineering, 1997–2026*

When the American Society of Civil Engineers added "Geoenvironmental" to the masthead in 1997, the journal it renamed was already the oldest continuous soil-mechanics publication in the world: born in 1956 as the *Journal of the Soil Mechanics and Foundations Division*, renamed the *Journal of the Geotechnical Engineering Division* in 1974 and the *Journal of Geotechnical Engineering* in 1983, it carries volume numbers inherited from ASCE's Proceedings — which is why the 1997 relaunch began at Volume 123 and the current volume is 152. This analysis covers the journal's thirty years under its present name and ISSN, 1997–2026: the era in which "JGGE" became the standard abbreviation on every geotechnical reference list.

To take stock, I assembled the journal's complete publication record from Volume 123 (1997) to Volume 152 (2026) — titles, authors, abstracts, keywords, institutional affiliations and citation counts. After setting aside discussions, closures, book reviews, editorials and errata (1,622 items — the journal's famously active discussion culture accounts for most of them), the analysed corpus comprises **4,863 research papers**, which together have accumulated roughly **274,000 citations**. The dataset was compiled from the OpenAlex and Crossref scholarly databases; details and caveats are in the methods note at the end.

What emerges is a journal quite unlike its British and Canadian counterparts in one respect — its output has been remarkably stable for three decades — and very much like them in another: the same twin rise of the constitutive model and the computer, the same march toward larger and more international teams, and the same eastward shift of authorship, arriving here later but unmistakably.

## A journal's life in numbers

![Annual research output of JGGE, 1997–2026](/images/posts/jgge-30-years/fig1_annual_output.svg)

*Figure 1. Annual research output of JGGE, 1997–2026.*

Where *Géotechnique* doubled and the *Canadian Geotechnical Journal* quintupled, JGGE has held a nearly constant line: roughly 110–175 research papers a year for most of its thirty years. The exceptions are instructive. The all-time record of **264 papers in 2009** was not a special issue but a uniformly fat year — every one of the twelve issues ran large. A second, sustained step up came in 2020–21 (232 and 233 papers), and the last two years have stayed high (199 in 2024, 213 in 2025). With 113 papers published by mid-August, 2026 is tracking the recent norm. The flat profile is a choice, not a symptom: ASCE has kept the journal's page budget disciplined while the field's submission volume — visible in the explosive growth of its competitors — rose around it.

## What the field studies — and how that has changed

Each paper was assigned to one or more of sixteen themes by matching its title, abstract and keywords against theme-specific vocabularies (a paper on the seismic response of piles counts toward both "earthquake" and "piles"). Because themes overlap, shares within a period do not sum to 100%.

![Share of papers touching each theme, by period](/images/posts/jgge-30-years/fig2_theme_heatmap.svg)

*Figure 2. Share of papers touching each theme, by period.*

**The constants.** The journal's backbone is the same one visible in every general geotechnics journal: groundwater, seepage and pore-pressure problems (26–34% in every period), embankments, dams and ground improvement (23–27% — with the levee, post-Katrina, as a distinctly American accent), laboratory testing, and piles (a steady 17–18%). Numerical and analytical methods have been the journal's single largest theme almost from the start — 30% already in 1997–2001, 38% in the 2020s — a reminder that by the time JGGE took its present name, the finite element method was already furniture; the recent increment is reliability analysis, random fields and machine learning.

![The big movers: themes with the largest change in share](/images/posts/jgge-30-years/fig3_big_movers.svg)

*Figure 3. The big movers: themes with the largest change in share.*

**The risers.**

- *Constitutive & soil behaviour* is the strongest riser: 17% of the journal in 1997–2001, **32% in the 2020s** and climbing — critical-state frameworks, micromechanics, DEM and particle-scale physics. The same theme, over the same interval, leads the rise in *Géotechnique* and the CGJ: the whole field has turned toward soil behaviour.
- *Laboratory testing* rose with it (24% → 33%, peaking at 36% in 2012–16), reflecting both the behaviour agenda and the American centrifuge network — "centrifuge" is one of the journal's most frequent keywords, with UC Davis and UWA among its most prolific institutions.
- *Offshore & energy* is the fastest proportional climber (4% → **16%**): monopiles, suction caissons, anchors and, lately, energy geo-structures and gas hydrates — the energy transition writ in soil.
- *Unsaturated soils & climate effects* doubled (12% → 21%), the Lu–Likos suction-stress school prominent within it.
- *Earthquake & liquefaction*, the journal's signature strength since the NCEER era, drifted upward from an already-high base (20% → 27–28%).

**The fallers.** Only two themes have genuinely declined: *rock mechanics* (8% → 4.5% — the material has its own journals) and, gently over the last decade, *tunnels & underground* (10% → 7%), which had risen through the 2000s urban-tunnelling boom before ASCE's own specialist outlets and the mega-growth of tunnelling elsewhere absorbed the flow. Notably, *environmental & bio-geotechnics* — the theme the 1997 renaming announced — has stayed between 10% and 16% throughout: the "geoenvironmental" half of the name describes a steady tenant, not a takeover. Its internal composition, though, has transformed: the landfill-liner and contaminant-transport papers of the late 1990s have given way to MICP, biopolymers and tailings.

## Who writes the journal: a geography transformed

Affiliation coverage is good across the whole window (91% of papers in 1997–2001, above 98% since), so the geography can be traced from the beginning: 4,863 papers, authors from 90 countries and roughly 2,200 institutions.

![Top 15 contributing countries, 1997–2026](/images/posts/jgge-30-years/fig4_top_countries.svg)

*Figure 4. Top 15 contributing countries, 1997–2026 (full counting).*

Over the full thirty years the United States towers over the table: 2,573 papers — 53% of the corpus — carry at least one US-affiliated author, more than the next seven countries combined. China (642 from the mainland, plus a further 281 papers carrying Hong Kong affiliations), Australia (395), the United Kingdom (378) and Canada (377) follow. But the totals conceal the trajectory:

![Country shares by period](/images/posts/jgge-30-years/fig5_country_share_by_era.svg)

*Figure 5. Where the papers come from: country shares by period.*

- **1997–2001:** the journal was, in effect, a national journal with guests — the US held 48% of country mentions, and no other country reached 6%.
- **2007–11:** the US share had eased to 37%; China entered the top four for the first time (5%).
- **2022–26:** the US is still first but at **25%**, with mainland China at **18%** — and greater China (adding Hong Kong's 6%) essentially level with the United States. The United Kingdom (8%) has quietly climbed to third.

This is the same eastward shift already documented in *Géotechnique* (where China overtook the UK) and the CGJ (where China overtook Canada) — JGGE is simply the last of the three flagships where the home country still leads, and on current trends the crossover is a few years away. As at the CGJ, the American share decline is dilution rather than retreat: US output per year has been broadly stable while the journal's non-US intake grew.

![Most prolific institutions, 1997–2026](/images/posts/jgge-30-years/fig7_top_institutions.svg)

*Figure 6. Most prolific institutions, 1997–2026.*

The institutional league table captures the journal's character: the top of it is a near-tie between the **Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (164 papers)**, **UC Davis (163)** — home of the US centrifuge programme and the bio-geotechnics school — and the **University of Western Australia (157)**, the offshore-geotechnics powerhouse. Berkeley, the Colorado School of Mines, Virginia Tech, Texas, Wisconsin and Georgia Tech fill out an American core, with Zhejiang and Cambridge the only other non-US entrants in the top dozen.

## From lone authors to global teams

![International co-authorship and team size by period](/images/posts/jgge-30-years/fig6_international_collaboration.svg)

*Figure 7. International co-authorship and team size by period.*

The sociology of the journal has changed as much as its science. The mean paper had 2.6 authors in the late 1990s and has **3.9** today; single-authored papers have gone from 15% of the journal to 2%. International co-authorship dipped to 28% in 2002–06 and has climbed every period since, to **47% in 2022–26** — nearly half the journal now crosses a border (the elevated 34% figure for 1997–2001 should be read with slight caution, as it is computed on the 91% of early papers with affiliation data). The drivers are familiar: shared centrifuges and field campaigns, international students who become international collaborators, and a field whose problems — liquefaction, offshore wind, tailings — are themselves borderless.

## What gets cited

The journal's citation record is topped by a document unlike any other in the geotechnical literature: the consensus report of the NCEER liquefaction workshops, with twenty-one authors and nearly 3,000 citations — for two decades the field's standard of practice for liquefaction evaluation.

The table reads like a map of the journal's distinctive strengths. Liquefaction takes two of the top ten (three counting the soil-pile-structure interaction work just below the cut); reliability and probabilistic methods take two more, marking JGGE as the home journal of geotechnical risk. Most striking is bio-geotechnics: the DeJong and van Paassen papers — both foundational MICP studies — belong to a theme that was only a few percent of the journal when they appeared, and J. Carlos Santamarina co-authors three of the top ten. The most-cited *recent* work continues both threads: particle-scale physics and bio-mediated ground improvement are the journal's biggest bets on its own future.

### Methods note

**Data.** The dataset was assembled from **OpenAlex** and **Crossref** records for ISSN 1090-0241/1943-5606 (retrieved August 2026) and merged by DOI. Document types were classified by title convention (ASCE's "Discussion of…", "Closure to…", "Erratum for…" formats, plus the all-caps ". DISCUSSION AND CLOSURE" style of the 1997–99 backfile) supplemented by database type fields; 19 double-registered records were merged, with citations summed. A further 134 DOI-less records from 1973–95 — strays from the predecessor *Journal of the Soil Mechanics and Foundations Division* and *Journal of the Geotechnical Engineering Division* misattributed to this source in OpenAlex — were excluded, as the predecessor titles are not covered here. OpenAlex systematically misresolves ASCE membership designations ("M.ASCE"), ORCID lines and several consulting-firm affiliations to spurious institutions; these were removed before counting.

**Themes.** Theme assignment used regular-expression vocabularies over titles, abstracts and keywords; themes overlap by design, so shares within a period do not sum to 100%.

**Geography.** Countries are credited by full counting (every country on a paper gets one credit); affiliation coverage is 91% in 1997–2001 and 98–100% thereafter, with missing affiliations filled where possible from raw affiliation strings. Hong Kong and Taiwan affiliations are tallied separately from mainland China's in the figures and are labelled China (Hong Kong) and China (Taiwan).

**Caveats.** Citation counts are OpenAlex, as of August 2026, and favour older papers; 2026 is a partial year (papers to mid-August). Theme vocabularies are heuristic and English-language. Institution names reflect OpenAlex normalisation.
