*A combined bibliometric analysis of 15,418 research papers, 1948–2026*

[Géotechnique](/posts/2026/08/geotechnique-78-years/) (founded 1948, London), the [Canadian Geotechnical Journal](/posts/2026/08/cgj-63-years/) (CGJ, founded 1963) and ASCE's [Journal of Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Engineering](/posts/2026/08/jgge-30-years/) (JGGE, current title since 1997) are, between them, the three most-cited general-purpose geotechnical engineering journals in the English language. Each has already been profiled on its own. Read together, their combined publication record — 19,066 items, of which **15,418 are research papers** (6,184 CGJ, 4,863 JGGE, 4,371 Géotechnique) spanning 78 years — tells a story none of the three tells alone: a single, increasingly globalised research community that happens to publish through three editorial boards on two continents, whose geography, team structure and thematic centre of gravity have moved in lockstep.

The centrepiece of this analysis is new: rather than just counting how many authors sign a paper, I reconstructed **who those authors' institutions actually are and where they sit**, and built a map of every institution-to-institution collaboration link the three journals contain — the geotechnical-research equivalent of an airline route map.

## Growth, combined

![Annual research output: JGGE + Géotechnique + Canadian Geotechnical Journal](/images/posts/three-journals-combined/fig1_annual_output.svg)

*Figure 1. Annual research output: JGGE + Géotechnique + Canadian Geotechnical Journal.*

Overlaying the three output curves shows a field expanding in three distinct waves: Géotechnique alone through the 1950s–60s, joined by CGJ from 1963, and by the modern JGGE (successor to the ASCE Journal of the Soil Mechanics and Foundations Division) from 1997. Combined annual output crossed 100 research papers a year in the early 1970s, 300 by 2000, and now runs at 400–530 papers a year — effectively one new geotechnics paper published across these three venues every day of the working year. CGJ has been the single largest contributor since the 2000s; JGGE and Géotechnique have run neck-and-neck behind it for the last decade.

## What the field studies, combined

Each paper was matched against the same seventeen theme vocabularies used in the individual journal analyses (CGJ's superset, which adds "Cold regions & permafrost"), so a paper can — and often does — touch more than one theme.

![Share of combined papers touching each theme, by decade](/images/posts/three-journals-combined/fig2_theme_heatmap.svg)

*Figure 2. Share of combined papers touching each theme, by decade.*

Pooling the three journals confirms that the field's big structural shifts are not artefacts of any one editorial board — they are the whole discipline moving together. **Numerical and analytical methods** is now the single most common theme across all three journals (30% of the combined corpus, up from 3% in 1948–59), overtaking even laboratory testing and groundwater/seepage, the two themes that have anchored the field since its founding. Constitutive and soil-behaviour modelling shows the same climb (6% → 32%).

![The big movers: themes with the largest change in share](/images/posts/three-journals-combined/fig3_big_movers.svg)

*Figure 3. The big movers: themes with the largest change in share.*

The fastest-moving themes combined are the same ones flagged in the individual journals, now visibly synchronised across all three mastheads: **offshore & energy** (0% before 1970, 15% since the 2020s, driven first by North Sea platforms and now offshore wind), **environmental & bio-geotechnics** (essentially absent before 1980, now 14%, on bio-cementation, tailings and sustainability), and **earthquake & liquefaction** (1% → 21%). Cold regions and permafrost — CGJ's signature theme — sits at a combined 3% overall but is naturally concentrated in CGJ, where the Canadian and circumpolar focus keeps it a recurring topic no other journal covers at scale.

## A geography that is tilting the same way everywhere

Restricting the geography and collaboration analysis to 2008–2026 — the period where affiliation deposition is reliably complete for all three journals (CGJ's pre-2008 coverage is under 20%) — leaves **8,149 research papers with usable affiliation data**, drawn from authors in over 3,600 distinct institutions across more than 100 countries.

![Top 15 contributing countries across the three journals](/images/posts/three-journals-combined/fig4_top_countries.svg)

*Figure 4. Top 15 contributing countries across the three journals, 2008–2026.*

Aggregated, the United States (2,419 country-mentions), China (2,224, counting Hong Kong and Taiwan as China throughout this analysis), Canada (1,067), the United Kingdom (1,032) and Australia (924) lead — unsurprising, since each is the historic home country of one of the three journals (or, for China, the country whose research output has grown the fastest everywhere in science over this period). China trails the US overall by less than 200 mentions out of over 15,000 combined papers, and, as the period breakdown below shows, has already overtaken the US in the most recent years. What is more interesting is what the three journals look like from the inside:

- **Géotechnique** (published in London): United Kingdom leads (451), but **China is already second (381)**, ahead of the journal's own historic second country, the United States (218).
- **CGJ** (published in Canada): **China is now the single largest contributor (1,060 country-mentions) — well ahead of Canada itself (726)**. A journal named for one country is, by this measure, majority non-domestic and led by another.
- **JGGE** (published in the US): the United States still leads comfortably (1,674), but China is a clear second (783), well ahead of the UK, Australia and Canada.

The pattern is identical in direction, only different in degree: every journal's own historic home country is losing relative share, and China is rising toward or past second place in all three, simultaneously.

![Where the papers come from: country shares by period](/images/posts/three-journals-combined/fig5_country_share_by_period.svg)

*Figure 5. Where the papers come from: country shares by period.*

The period breakdown makes the trajectory unambiguous: combined, China's share of country-mentions rises from 12% (2008–13) to 17% (2014–19) to **25% (2020–26)**, overtaking the United States — whose share falls from 25% to 18% over the same span — as the single largest source of country-mentions in the most recent period. Canada, the UK and Australia each hold roughly steady in the high single digits.

![Most prolific institutions across the three journals](/images/posts/three-journals-combined/fig7_top_institutions.svg)

*Figure 6. Most prolific institutions across the three journals, 2008–2026.*

The combined institutional table reflects each journal's regional gravity blended together: the University of Western Australia (offshore geotechnics), Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Zhejiang University, Cambridge, the University of Hong Kong, Queen's University (Canada) and Hong Kong Polytechnic University lead the table — five of the top seven institutions are now in Greater China or built around China-facing collaboration, and the Norwegian Geotechnical Institute is the most prolific contributor from continental Europe.

## The world map of who works with whom

Country-level counting answers *where* the field's authors sit, but it hides the actual social structure of the collaborations — which specific institutions, in which specific cities, actually write papers together. To answer that, I went back to the raw affiliation records for every 2008–2026 research paper, resolved each institution's home city via the OpenAlex institutions database (3,704 unique institutions, 99.6% successfully geolocated), and built a weighted edge for every pair of distinct-city co-affiliations that appear on the same paper — exactly the data structure behind a commercial airline route map, where cities are airports and co-authored papers are flights between them.

Three things stand out immediately:

**The hub cities are university towns, not capitals.** Unlike a real airline map, where hub strength tracks population and air-transport infrastructure, this map's biggest nodes are wherever a country's leading geotechnical engineering department happens to sit. **Hong Kong** is the single busiest node in the entire network (741 combined collaboration links) — a remarkable result for a city of 7.5 million, reflecting the sheer density of geotechnical research capacity packed into HKUST, HKU and PolyU and their intensive mainland-China collaboration pipeline. **Beijing** (611), **London** (575, Imperial + UCL combined) and **Nanjing** (575, Hohai University) follow, then a second tier of **Hangzhou** (Zhejiang University), **Wuhan**, **Perth** (University of Western Australia — the offshore-geotechnics capital of the Southern Hemisphere), **Singapore** and **Shanghai**. Famous global transit hubs that dominate real flight-route maps — New York, Amsterdam, Dubai — barely register here: geotechnical research collaboration follows university strength, not airport connectivity.

**The busiest individual routes are regional, not transoceanic.** The single heaviest link in the whole dataset is Hong Kong–Shenzhen (65 co-authored papers) — two cities 30km apart either side of the mainland/SAR border — followed by Hong Kong–Nanjing (46) and Beijing–Hong Kong (41). Five of the ten busiest city-pairs sit entirely within China. This is the signature of a research system where dense, geographically-compact collaboration networks (the Yangtze River Delta cities of Nanjing, Hangzhou, Shanghai and Wuhan chief among them) have grown around a small number of internationally-facing hubs, rather than every city collaborating equally with every other city.

**International collaboration keeps climbing.** Of the 8,149 affiliation-complete papers, 37.1% list authors from at least two different countries (with Hong Kong and Taiwan counted as China, so mainland–Hong Kong and mainland–Taiwan collaboration — a large share of the network's busiest city-pairs, above — counts as domestic rather than international) — up from 31.2% in 2008–13 to 44.5% in 2020–26. At the country-pair level the busiest single links are China–United States (380 co-authored papers), China–United Kingdom (220), Canada–United States (194), Australia–China (171) and United Kingdom–United States (162): the same handful of national pairs that dominate international science generally, reproduced almost exactly inside geotechnical engineering.

![International co-authorship by journal, and team size over time](/images/posts/three-journals-combined/fig6_collaboration_and_team_size.svg)

*Figure 8. International co-authorship by journal, and team size over time.*

International co-authorship (2008–2026) is broadly similar across the three journals — 43.7% for Géotechnique, 37.6% for CGJ, 38.8% for JGGE — suggesting this is a field-wide phenomenon rather than a quirk of any one journal's author pool. Team size has grown steadily and identically everywhere: the mean climbed from 1.2 authors per paper in 1948–59 to **4.0 in the 2020s**, while the share of single-author papers collapsed from 72% in the journals' earliest years to under 3% today.

## Indications

1. **This is one research community with three mastheads, not three research communities.** Every structural trend identified separately in each journal — the rise of numerical/constitutive methods, the offshore-energy and bio-geotechnics waves, the growth in team size and international co-authorship — appears in near-identical form and timing across all three. A paper's choice of journal looks increasingly like a matter of author nationality and editorial-board relationships rather than differences in what the field studies.

2. **China's rise is not a Géotechnique story, a CGJ story or a JGGE story — it is happening to all three simultaneously**, and in CGJ's case has already overtaken the journal's own country of publication. This is the single most consistent finding across the combined dataset.

3. **Collaboration has a geography of its own, distinct from output.** The busiest collaboration hub (Hong Kong) is not the largest single-country contributor (the US); the busiest individual routes are short regional links inside China's Yangtze Delta and Pearl River Delta, not the long transoceanic links a citation count alone would suggest. Understanding *where the field publishes from* and *who actually works with whom* are genuinely different questions, and this dataset is the first attempt to answer the second one at this scale for geotechnical engineering.

4. **International collaboration keeps climbing.** At 44.5% international co-authorship in 2020–26 (from 31.2% just over a decade earlier, and counting Hong Kong and Taiwan as China rather than as separate countries), cross-border collaboration is closing in on the norm rather than the exception across the combined literature.

### Methods note

**Data.** This analysis merges the three journals' independently-compiled datasets (each retrieved from the OpenAlex and Crossref APIs, deduplicated for double DOI registrations, and classified into research vs. non-research items by title/type rules — see each journal's own methods note for details specific to that title: [Géotechnique](/posts/2026/08/geotechnique-78-years/), [CGJ](/posts/2026/08/cgj-63-years/), [JGGE](/posts/2026/08/jgge-30-years/)). The combined corpus is 19,066 items, of which 15,418 (81%) are research papers.

**Themes, countries and periods.** Themes, country normalisation and era/period definitions reuse the same regular-expression theme vocabularies, ISO-country mapping and non-research classification rules already validated on each journal individually; the combined analysis simply pools the three cleaned per-journal datasets and re-runs the aggregation. Unlike the earlier per-journal posts, which tallied Hong Kong and Taiwan separately from mainland China and relabelled them "China (Hong Kong)"/"China (Taiwan)" in the figures, this combined analysis counts Hong Kong and Taiwan as China outright, throughout — in country-level totals, period shares and country-pair collaboration links alike. A paper with co-authors in, say, mainland China and Hong Kong therefore counts once toward China's total and is not counted as international collaboration, while it still appears in full in the city-level co-authorship map below (Hong Kong remains its own city node there, as does every mainland Chinese city).

**The world co-authorship map** is new for this combined analysis and required going back to the raw OpenAlex authorship records (rather than the already-flattened per-paper country/institution columns), because a city-level map needs to know *which* institutions co-appear on the same paper, not just which countries are present. For each 2008–2026 research paper, every author's listed institutions were resolved to an OpenAlex institution ID; three IDs already known to be misresolved parsing artefacts specific to JGGE's ASCE-style author bylines ("ORCID", "American Society of Civil Engineers", and "GEI Consultants" — which OpenAlex incorrectly attaches to a grab-bag of unrelated consulting firms and even a Bethesda hospital via a shared administrative address) were excluded, matching the filtering already applied in JGGE's own per-journal pipeline. Each institution's city and coordinates were then bulk-fetched from the OpenAlex institutions endpoint (3,704 unique institutions, 99.6% resolved); the University of Hong Kong's district-level geocode ("Pok Fu Lam") was folded back into "Hong Kong" as the same city. For every paper, an edge was added between every pair of *distinct* cities appearing among its authors' institutions (weight = number of co-authored papers linking that city pair); country-level edges were built the same way. Analysis restricted to 2008–2026 for consistency with the rest of the geography analysis (CGJ's pre-2008 affiliation coverage is below 20%). The static figure shows only links with ≥4 shared papers and labels the 14 strongest, spatially-declustered hub cities; the interactive map above shows links with ≥2 shared papers and all hover detail.

**Caveats.** Citation counts are OpenAlex, as of the underlying per-journal analyses. Theme vocabularies are heuristic and English-language. Institution and country normalisation follow the rules set out above; a small number of institutions (0.4%) could not be geolocated and are excluded from the world map.
